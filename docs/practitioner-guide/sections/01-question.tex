\section{What the calculation answers}
\label{ch:question}

\subsection{The policy experiment}

Suppose an analyst can lower the underlying transport cost on one network
link. Which link produces the largest economic benefit? A traditional answer
multiplies the cost reduction by the traffic using that link. This is the
social-savings calculation associated with \citet{fogel1962quantitative} and
\citet{Fogel1964Railroads}. It is transparent and often informative. It also
raises a natural question: what happens when users change routes, switch
modes, move across locations, or change the congestion faced by other users?

The package evaluates a local version of that question. A policy is a small
change in the primitive cost of a declared edge--mode. For a directed road
edge \(e=(k,l)\), mode \(m\), write
\[
  \theta_{klm}=\log\bar\kappa_{kl,m},
\]
where \(\bar\kappa_{kl,m}\) is the cost supplied by infrastructure before
endogenous congestion. The main output is
\[
  -\frac{d\log W}{d\theta_{klm}},
\]
the elasticity of aggregate welfare with respect to a reduction in that
primitive cost. A one-percent improvement is the derivative multiplied by
0.01. The software reports the derivative itself and records the shock size in
the run manifest.

The distinction between a directed edge and a physical link matters. A
directed policy changes one direction. A physical-link policy changes the two
reciprocal directions simultaneously and sums their first-order effects. The
package never treats an unpaired directed edge as bidirectional.

\subsection{Traditional and extended measures}

Let \(\Xi_{kl,m}\) be value traffic on the edge--mode, normalized by world
income. The traditional statistic is
\[
  H_{klm}=\Xi_{kl,m}.
\]
It is a welfare elasticity, not a raw vehicle count. Multiplying by world
income converts the share to currency units before applying a small
proportional cost change.

The extended statistic lets routes, modes, congestion, prices, and the
distribution of economic activity respond. It is still a local derivative.
The calculation does not solve a finite construction project, assign a
construction cost, or predict a transition path. It asks how welfare changes
at the observed equilibrium when one primitive cost changes marginally.

This local focus has three advantages. First, it separates the economic
mechanism from assumptions about project scale. Second, the same derivative
can rank many candidate links. Third, the adjoint method computes all link
derivatives after one equilibrium linearization. The limitation is equally
important: a large project can leave the neighborhood in which the derivative
is accurate. Section \ref{ch:diagnostics} explains how to compare the
first-order prediction with finite changes.

\subsection{Benefits rather than a complete appraisal}

The welfare measure covers channels represented in the model: resource costs
of moving goods, route and mode substitution, congestion, spatial
externalities, and changes in the location of economic activity. It does not
include a construction budget, maintenance, financing, pollution, safety,
neighborhood disruption, displacement, segregation, or distributional
incidence. These objects can be important and should be reported separately.

\warningbox{Do not label the output ``net social benefit'' unless every
material project cost and externality has been brought into the welfare
criterion. In the paper application, ``model-implied economic benefit'' is the
accurate description.}

The package therefore complements conventional transport appraisal rather
than replacing it. Conventional analysis supplies engineering costs, demand
evidence, safety effects, and local externalities. The spatial statistic shows
how observed network use and equilibrium adjustment alter the benefit side of
that ledger. Useful introductions to conventional practice include
\citet{SmallVerhoefLindsey2024} and \citet{dePalmaEtAl2011Handbook}; spatial
channels are reviewed by \citet{ReddingTurner2015} and
\citet{donaldson2025transport}.

\subsection{A sufficient-statistic workflow}

The application is best understood as a sufficient-statistic exercise. The
inputs are model-ready labor and income shares, directed value flows by mode,
network incidence, and a small set of elasticities. From these inputs the
software reconstructs the recursive routing system and the exposure of each
location to inward and outward markets. It does not estimate the entire model
from raw administrative records.

The practical workflow is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item state the policy unit and the primitive being changed;
  \item construct a directed, mode-specific value-flow network;
  \item verify the accounting identities required by the recursive model;
  \item choose and document the spatial, modal, and congestion
  specifications;
  \item validate the baseline and condition numbers;
  \item compute traditional and extended elasticities;
  \item inspect the decomposition and numerical diagnostics;
  \item assess sensitivity and compare the ranking with external policy
  evidence.
\end{enumerate}

The order matters. A derivative from an inconsistent flow network is not made
credible by a sophisticated solver.
